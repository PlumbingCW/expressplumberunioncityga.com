How to Repipe a House With PEX, Step by Step

A PEX repipe replaces the old supply lines with flexible cross-linked polyethylene tubing. The work runs in a set order. The plumber maps the layout, picks a tubing type and fitting system, installs a manifold or a trunk line, runs the tubing to each fixture, pressure-tests everything, and then closes the walls.

The tubing is easy to handle, but the job is still regulated work. In Union City, permits go to the certified contractor, and the city enforces the 2018 International Plumbing Code with Georgia amendments. This guide walks through each choice and explains where a homeowner can help and where the work belongs to a licensed plumber.

What PEX is and why plumbers use it for repipes

PEX is polyethylene whose molecules are cross-linked, which makes it flexible and tough. It bends around corners, so a long run needs far fewer elbows than copper does. Fewer joints means fewer places a leak can start.

It is also less likely to burst from freezing than copper or rigid plastic pipe, because the tubing can expand slightly. That matters in Georgia, where hard freezes are rare but do happen. Metro Atlanta saw burst pipes and water main breaks in the January 2024 cold snap, and a master plumber told local news that many Georgia homes have little pipe insulation. PEX is not freeze-proof, so insulate exposed runs anyway.

PEX-A, PEX-B, and PEX-C are not the same

The letters describe how the tubing is cross-linked during manufacturing. All three are sold for potable water, but they handle a little differently on the job.

PEX types compared
TypeHow it is madeWhat installers notice
PEX-APeroxide method, cross-linked while hotVery flexible and kink-resistant, and works with expansion fittings
PEX-BSilane method, cured with moisture after extrusionA bit stiffer, widely sold, and commonly used with crimp or clamp rings
PEX-CElectron beam, cross-linked while coldLess uniform above 1 inch, and handled more carefully

The right pick depends on the fitting system, the tubing available, and the plumber's tools. Mixing brands of tubing and fittings is a common error, so the whole system should match the manufacturer's listed pairings.

Choosing a layout, manifold or trunk and branch

Layout is the biggest design decision. It changes how many fittings go in the walls, how fast hot water arrives, and where shutoffs live.

Home-run manifold

A central manifold feeds a separate line to every fixture. Each fixture gets its own shutoff, and fewer joints end up inside walls.

Trunk and branch

A larger trunk line runs through the house with smaller branches to fixtures. It resembles traditional copper plumbing and uses less tubing.

Hybrid

A manifold serves distant bathrooms while a short trunk covers fixtures clustered together. Many houses suit this mix.

A manifold uses more tubing, but it lets you shut off one sink without losing the whole bathroom. Trunk and branch costs less in materials and delivers pressure evenly when sized correctly. Ask your plumber to explain the choice for your floor plan instead of defaulting to one.

Fittings, crimp, clamp, expansion, and push-fit

The fitting system is where PEX jobs succeed or fail. Each method needs its own tool, and installers must follow the manufacturer's steps exactly.

  • Crimp rings are squeezed on with a crimp tool, and a go/no-go gauge checks every joint.
  • Clamp rings are cinched with a clamp tool, which suits tight spaces and reduces the need for a gauge.
  • Expansion fittings use a tool that stretches the tubing end so a ring and fitting seat inside it, and the tubing then shrinks back tight.
  • Push-fit connectors need no special tool and are handy for repairs, but they cost more per joint and are often kept out of walls.
  • Compression fittings tighten with wrenches and can be taken apart, which makes them popular for fixture stops.

Each system needs its own tool kit, so a plumber who owns the right tools for the chosen fitting is a good sign. A bad crimp leaks behind drywall, so every joint should be tested before it is closed up.

Chemicals, sunlight, and brass, the cautions that matter

PEX breaks down in sunlight. Store it away from direct light and shield it after installation. Some tubing includes UV blockers, but those give only limited protection, so a long run near an unshaded window or in a bright garage should be sleeved or covered.

Chemicals can also shorten its life. Tubing contains antioxidants that help it resist chlorine, but some adhesives and sealants on the pipe surface can weaken those antioxidants. Foam pipe wrap has not been linked to that problem. Use only products labeled safe for PEX when you seal around it.

Brass fittings count too. Yellow brass with about 30% zinc led to dezincification failures in Nevada, and red brass with 5 to 10% zinc replaced it. A related product called Kitec was discontinued after widespread failures tied to brass fittings. Ask what fitting alloy your plumber is installing.

How a PEX repipe runs on site

  1. Shut off the main, drain the lines, and photograph every fixture location.
  2. Install the manifold or trunk near the water heater, with room to reach shutoffs.
  3. Pull the tubing through wall cavities, attic, or crawlspace, using sleeves where it passes through framing.
  4. Make connections to fixtures, the water heater, and the main with the matching fitting system.
  5. Pressure-test the system and check every joint before walls are closed.
  6. Patch drywall, label the manifold valves, and give the owner a map of the lines.

A whole-house repipe on a typical home often takes more than one working day, with the water off for much of it. Slab houses add a rerouting step, since the old buried lines are usually capped and abandoned. Our repiping service page explains how that scope is set.

Licensing, permits, and where DIY stops

The tubing is friendly, and plenty of homeowners can cut and crimp a fixture stop. A whole-house repipe is different. It ties into the main, the water heater, and every fixture, and a hidden leak can ruin a ceiling before it shows.

In Georgia, plumbers are licensed through the State Division of Master and Journeyman Plumbers. Class I Master licenses cover single-family homes and dwellings up to two families. Class II Master has no size limits. You can check any plumber by name in the state licensee search before you sign anything.

The city says permits are issued to the certified contractor. That suggests a homeowner may not be able to pull the permit alone, so ask Union City Community Development before you buy a roll of tubing. A repipe done without a permit can raise questions during a sale or an insurance claim.

New builds and remodels, a different case

PEX shows up in new work too. When a bathroom is added or a kitchen is gutted, a plumber can run new lines before the walls close, which is cheaper in labor than opening finished rooms. If you are planning an addition, new plumbing installation covers the rough-in and fixture side.

Landlords who repipe a rental should ask for the manifold map and keep it with the lease file. Tenants should report low pressure or discolored water in writing so the repair is tied to a date.

Sharing the work with a licensed plumber

Homeowners can legitimately help by clearing access, moving furniture, and labeling shutoffs. Drywall patching and painting can be separate trades. The tie-ins, pressure test, and permit belong to the licensed contractor.

If you own a similar house in South Fulton or Palmetto, the same steps apply. Each place has its own permit office, so ask the local authority which rules cover your address.

PEX and code

PEX is an approved supply material under the International Plumbing Code (IPC), the base of Georgia's state plumbing code, when it is listed to the right standard and installed with matching fittings. Ask your plumber which listing marks are on the pipe and how the permit is handled in Union City.
