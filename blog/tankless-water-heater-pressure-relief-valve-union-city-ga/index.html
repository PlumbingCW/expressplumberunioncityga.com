Tankless Water Heater Pressure Relief Valve Installation in Union City, GA

Yes, a tankless water heater needs a pressure relief valve. Rinnai's installation manual states that an approved pressure relief valve is required by the American National Standard ANSI Z21.10.3 for all water heating systems, and the valve ships in the box with its units. Other brands publish their own rules, so your model's manual comes first.

The same manual adds a detail that trips people up. The standard does not require a combination temperature and pressure relief valve on that appliance, but local codes may. Below you will find what the valve does, how the discharge pipe must run, why a valve drips, and how hard water affects the whole setup.

What a relief valve does on a tankless unit

A relief valve is the last line of defense when pressure builds in the hot water side. Rheem's valve instructions say the purpose is to help protect against excessive pressure and temperature, and that the valve opens and discharges water if either condition develops. It is a spring-loaded safety part, not a control, so it should stay closed until something goes wrong.

Tankless heaters hold very little water compared with a tank, but the plumbing around them still holds pressure. Heated water expands, and it has nowhere to go if the system is sealed. The valve gives that pressure a safe exit instead of letting it stress the heat exchanger and the pipes.

Discharge pipe rules that installers follow

Water that leaves a relief valve can scald instantly, so where the pipe ends matters as much as the valve itself. Rinnai's manual sets out the rules below, and the Rheem and A.O. Smith documents agree on most of them for tank models.

  • Pipe the discharge to the ground or into a drain system, as local codes direct.
  • Pitch the line downward and end it 6 inches above a drain, where any discharge is clearly visible.
  • Finish the pipe with a plain, unthreaded end at least 3/4 inch nominal in diameter.
  • Use pipe rated for water at 180 degrees Fahrenheit or hotter.
  • Put no other valve or shutoff between the relief valve and the heater, and none in the line.
  • Never plug, cap, or restrict the line, and do not add reducing fittings.
  • Plumb the relief valve and the condensate drain separately, each to its own drain.
  • Protect the valve and the discharge line from freezing.

A discharge line that stops short, points at a wall cavity, or ends in a threaded cap is a defect, not a style choice. If you can see your valve, look at the pipe now. It should be open at the bottom, clearly visible, and clear of live electrical parts.

Why a tankless relief valve drips

Rinnai's manual says periodic discharge can come from thermal expansion in a closed water supply system. A closed system forms when the meter or house has a check valve, backflow preventer, or pressure reducing valve. The manual tells owners to contact the water supplier or local plumbing inspector about correcting it, and never to plug the valve.

A valve that has started to leak should be replaced, not tightened or sealed. If it is weeping, a water heater repair visit can handle the swap. Rheem's instructions state that repair or alteration of the valve is prohibited by national safety standards.

Testing the valve once a year

Rinnai and Rheem both call for working the valve by hand once a year. Lift the lever far enough to send water to the drain, hold it for 5 to 10 seconds, and let it snap shut. Water should flow freely and then stop. Stand clear of the discharge line first, since the water may be extremely hot.

If the valve keeps dripping after you release the lever, close the cold supply to the heater and call a plumber. Do not try to clean or adjust it yourself.

Hard water, scale, and the relief valve

Scale is the quiet enemy of tankless heaters. Rinnai's manual says that in an area with hard water, the water must be treated and may need a more frequent flushing schedule. It also notes that scale builds faster when the heater runs at a high temperature setting.

The unit includes isolation valves for flushing the heat exchanger and a Service Soon code that tells the owner when to flush. Skipping that service lets scale build up and cut flow through the heat exchanger. If your heater throws a service code, follow the maker's flush steps or call for service.

Can you install the relief valve yourself?

Rinnai's manual states that a trained and qualified professional must install the appliance, inspect it, and leak test it, and that improper installation voids the warranty. The relief valve is part of that package. Hiring the whole job out keeps the valve, the pipe, and the warranty on the same page.

What you can do is look. Check that the discharge line is open, visible, and pointed downward, and that no one has stored items against it. When a new heater is on your list, our tankless water heater service covers the install, and you can call (770) 214-4545 to ask about your model.

What to ask on a tankless quote

  • Which relief valve does the manual call for, and does local code want a combination T&P valve?
  • Where will the discharge line end, and is the end visible?
  • Is my water system closed, and is an expansion tank part of the job?
  • What is the flushing plan for scale, and are isolation valves included?
  • Who handles the permit, if one is needed?

The same questions apply for homeowners in Palmetto and Fairburn, where hard water and permit rules differ from town to town. Ask each contractor to answer in writing so you can compare.

What to do if the relief valve is discharging

Never plug or cap the valve. Turn off power or gas to the unit, close the cold supply, and keep people away from the discharge, because hot water can scald. Then call a plumber.
