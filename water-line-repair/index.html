Emergency Water Line Repair in Union City, GA

Emergency water line repair in Union City, GA means fixing the buried pipe that carries city water from the meter to your house. A break there can mean a soggy yard, no water at the tap, or a meter that will not stop. This page follows the job in order, from finding the line to the final pressure test.

Where the line runs and who owns which part

The water service line starts at the meter, usually in a box near the curb, and runs underground to the main shutoff valve at your house. It may cross a lawn, a walkway or a driveway on the way. Depth, material and route differ from lot to lot, so the first step in any repair is learning where yours actually goes.

Union City buys its water from the City of Atlanta and maintains its own distribution system of about 112 miles of water line. Its leak guidance says a leak on the property owner's side of the meter is the owner's responsibility, and a leak on the city's side is repaired by a city crew. Ask the city water department where its responsibility ends on your lot and confirm the answer on unioncityga.org, because it decides who pays and who digs.

If the break is in the street, at the meter, or in the main under the road, call Public Services at (770) 306-6855 and report it. If it is between the meter and the house, the private line is the plumber's job.

When a water line break is an emergency

Treat it as an emergency when water is bubbling up in the yard or driveway, the ground is sinking or turning to mud, the house has lost water or pressure suddenly, or the meter is running hard with everything off. A line that is leaking at that rate can wash soil away from the pipe and undercut a walk or slab. Pooling water near a house wall can also seep into the foundation area.

  • Close the main shutoff valve at the house. If the leak continues, the break is likely before the valve.
  • Do not walk or park over soft ground near the leak, since soil around a broken pipe can give way.
  • Check the meter. If it still moves with the house valve closed, the leak is on the buried line.
  • Photograph the wet area and the meter reading, with the date visible if you can.
  • Call (770) 214-4545. Tell the dispatcher where the water is surfacing and whether the meter is moving.

Pinpointing the break before anyone digs

A wet spot in the yard is not always on top of the break. Water follows the line, loose soil and rock, and it can surface some distance from where the pipe actually failed. Digging at the wettest spot is the surest way to make a bigger hole than necessary.

Instead, the plumber closes the house valve and runs the meter test to confirm the leak is on the buried line. Ground microphones and a correlator then listen for the leak noise along the route, and tracer gas can pinpoint a quiet leak in plastic pipe. The leak detection page explains these tools in more detail. A marked spot, with a short list of what sits on top of it, becomes the basis for the dig plan.

Georgia 811 before the shovel goes in

Buried gas, electric, telecom and water lines share the same ground, and a hit can cause injury, outages or a gas leak. Georgia 811 connects excavators and homeowners with the member utilities that mark their lines for free. Its own site says state law does not require a notice for small, non-mechanized digging, but it recommends calling anyway, including for shovel work.

A trench for a water line is mechanical digging, so call 811 first, and ask the plumber to confirm that a locate ticket has been placed. Marks only cover the utilities that belong to members, so a private irrigation line or an old pipe can still be unmarked. Expect to wait for marks before a planned repair, though a flooding emergency may allow shutting water off while locates are arranged.

Trenchless work leaves most of the surface intact, but it still needs entry pits and a locate. The plumber should explain why one method suits your route, what the lawn will look like afterward, and what happens to the old pipe.

Tie-in, pressure test and backfill

Once the failed section is out, new pipe is joined to the meter side and the house side with fittings that match the material. Copper, PEX and polyethylene lines each use different connectors, so the plumber confirms the pipe type before choosing them. A shutoff valve at the house end lets you isolate the line later without calling anyone.

Before the trench is closed, the new line is filled and tested under pressure. A leak shows as a falling gauge or a wet fitting, which is far cheaper to fix with the trench still open. Then the soil goes back in layers, compacted so the surface does not sink, and the area is cleaned and graded.

Low water pressure from the supply side or your own plumbing

Low pressure after a water line leak is common, since escaping water never reaches your fixtures. Low pressure with no visible leak has other causes, including a failing pressure regulator, a half-closed valve, corroded galvanized pipe, or a supply issue outside your home. A simple gauge test sorts out which one is likely.

  1. Shut off all water in the house, including the ice maker and irrigation.
  2. Screw a pressure gauge onto an outdoor hose bib. Gauges are sold at hardware stores.
  3. Open the bib fully and read the needle.
  4. Compare the number with the guide below, then check a second tap to see if the reading matches.

Utility guidance such as the Jurupa Community Services District's pressure page treats 40 to 60 psi as acceptable for a home, calls anything under 40 low, and says a regulator is required above 80 psi. Those numbers are a guide, not a Union City rule.

A pressure-reducing valve, usually a bell-shaped brass fitting near the main valve, holds the house at a steady level. If the reading is high, a worn regulator may be letting full street pressure through, which strains pipes and fixtures. Ask the city water department what pressure to expect at your meter, and have a plumber test the regulator before blaming the supply.

Water main replacement, meter box and yard leaks

Water main replacement refers to the large pipes under the street, and those belong to the city's system. Homeowners do not repair them. What you can do is report the break, and ask for the status if your own line was damaged by the work.

A leaking meter box is a different matter. Union City's guidance says steady flow from the box can point to a leak, while intermittent flow may be irrigation or construction runoff. For city-side questions it asks residents to call Public Services at (770) 306-6855.

For a yard leak on the private line, the steps above apply, and a leak under the house floor is covered on the slab leak repair page. If the whole line is old, ask whether a repipe of the interior would follow.

Where your side begins

In Union City the service line past the meter is the property owner's side. Work on it is covered by the International Plumbing Code (IPC), and digging requires a Georgia 811 locate first. Ask Union City Public Services at (770) 306-6855 where the meter stops and your line begins.

What to do when the service line leaks

Close the main valve at the house and note the meter reading. Stay off saturated ground near the break, and do not dig yourself, since buried utilities must be marked through Georgia 811 first.

What changes the price of a water line repair

Length and depth of the line

A short spot repair costs less than replacing the full run between meter and house.

What sits on top

Driveways, walks, trees and landscaping change both the dig and the restoration.

Trench or trenchless

Trenchless methods need specialized equipment and entry pits, while open trenches need more surface repair.

Pipe material

Copper, PEX and polyethylene differ in cost, and old galvanized or mixed lines often need replacing.

Locates and timing

Waiting for 811 marks can slow a planned job, while an emergency may mean shutting water off first.

Some homeowners policies limit coverage for underground service lines, and many treat wear and slow leaks differently from a sudden break. Ask your insurer if your policy covers the line. The city's ServLine Protection Program covers part of a water bill after certain leaks are repaired, so ask the finance office if it applies.
