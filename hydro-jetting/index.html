Emergency Hydro Jetting in Union City, GA

What a hydro jetter physically does

A hydro jetter is a high-pressure water pump on a truck, trailer or cart, connected to a reel of reinforced hose. A steel nozzle on the end of the hose has jets facing forward and backward. The rear jets pull the hose up the pipe, and the forward and side jets break and flush what they meet.

Trade sources describe typical sewer jetting as roughly 50 to 80 gallons per minute at 2,500 to 3,500 psi. Psi is pressure, the force on each square inch of water. Gallons per minute is flow, the volume that carries debris out of the pipe. A good job needs both, because pressure cuts a blockage and flow moves the loosened material.

The plumber feeds the hose through a cleanout or an open fixture, sets the pressure to match the pipe, and works the nozzle through the line in passes. Water flows back toward the entry point and carries the cuttings with it. A cable cuts a hole through a clog. A jetter washes the full diameter of the wall.

Nozzles, pressure and flow in plain terms

The nozzle does the real work, and the right one depends on the blockage. A penetrating nozzle has strong forward jets for getting through a hard stoppage. A flushing or cleaning nozzle has jets angled back along the pipe wall. A rotating nozzle spins to cover the full circle and is a common choice for grease, scale and fine roots.

Nozzle types and what each does
Nozzle typeHow the jets are setBest used for
Stoppage (penetrating)Forward jets with rear thrust jetsBreaking through a solid blockage
CleaningRadial jets at a higher angle, about 21 to 45 degreesScouring the full wall of smaller pipes
FlushingRadial jets at a low angle, about 6 to 20 degreesMoving settled debris from the pipe bottom
RotationalRevolving head that sweeps the full circleGrease, scale and fibrous roots
Cutting or chain flailRotating blades or chainsThick roots and very hard scale, used with care

More force is not always better. Pipe makers set limits on pressure, jet angle and dwell time, and flexible materials such as PVC are damaged more easily than thick cast iron. Pressure is therefore a choice based on the pipe in front of the plumber, not a number to maximize.

Snaked it and it came back? The cases jetting is meant for

Most people call about jetting after another method has failed. The same four stories come up again and again, and each one has a reason jetting fits.

The clog returns in a few weeks

A cable opens a narrow channel through grease and scale. The coating on the wall stays, so the opening closes up. Jetting removes the coating instead of tunneling through it.

The cable got stuck or only went so far

A cable can hang up in a root mass or at an offset joint. A camera shows what it hit, and a cutting nozzle can then clear roots if the pipe is sound.

Roots keep growing back

Roots enter at joints and cracks where warm wastewater leaks out. Jetting cuts and flushes them, but the opening they used is still there, so regrowth is likely until the pipe is repaired or lined.

A kitchen line is full of grease

Cooled fat sticks to the pipe wall and builds in layers. Hot, high-flow water with a rotating nozzle breaks that layer up and flushes it out, which a cable cannot do.

Our drain cleaning page explains when a plain cable is enough. Jetting is the next step when the pattern says the buildup is on the walls and not in a single spot.

When hydro jetting is the wrong tool

Jetting cleans a pipe. It does not repair one. If a line is cracked, collapsed, belled out or separated at a joint, a strong water jet can wash soil into the break or make the damage worse. Flexible plastic pipe can also be harmed by the wrong nozzle at too much pressure.

Camera first when the pipe condition is unknown

If the line has never been inspected, or the cable has already snagged or come back muddy, we recommend a camera pass before jetting. A sewer camera inspection shows cracks, offsets and sags in minutes. It also gives you a recording to keep for a landlord, an insurer or a repair estimate.

A camera is also how we know when jetting is the wrong move. If the video shows a break or a belly full of standing water, the honest answer is a repair, not another wash. You hear that before any pressure goes in.

When a jetting call cannot wait

  • Sewage is coming up in a tub, shower or floor drain and the cleared line fills again within a day or two.
  • A kitchen line has backed up more than once and the dishwasher or sink cannot drain.
  • Multiple fixtures gurgle, drain slowly or back up when the washer runs.
  • A rental unit or a small business has lost its restroom or kitchen drain.
  • A cable was run earlier and the line is blocked again, with no camera view of why.

What to do while the jetting crew is on the way

  1. Stop all water use, including the dishwasher and washer, so the line does not push more water into the backup.
  2. Find the cleanout. It is a capped pipe, often white plastic or black iron, near the house foundation or in a utility room.
  3. Clear a path from the cleanout to the driveway so a hose can run without moving furniture or kids' toys.
  4. Take photos of any water or sewage in the house, with a date visible on your phone.
  5. If you rent, notify the landlord or manager by text or email so there is a written record.

Keep children and pets out of the area where sewage has reached the floor. Wear gloves for any cleanup and do not mix cleaning products, since some combinations release harmful fumes.

What changes the price of hydro jetting

Pipe size and length

A forty-foot four-inch branch is a smaller job than a long main with several bends. Longer runs need more hose and time.

What is in the pipe

Thin grease takes one pass. Thick scale or dense roots take several, and the plumber may change nozzles in between.

Access

An open cleanout lets the hose go in directly. If there is none, the crew may have to pull a toilet or open a vent, which adds labor.

Camera work

A before and after video adds time but protects you from jetting a damaged pipe and gives you a record.

When you call

Nights, weekends and holidays are emergency hours. Same-day appointments when available cost less than a late-night dispatch.

Insurers usually treat cleaning as maintenance and may not pay for it. A sudden backup that damages floors may be covered if your policy includes sewer or water backup coverage. Ask your insurer which endorsements you have and keep the camera recording and invoice.
